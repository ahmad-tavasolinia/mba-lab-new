## On How Honda Conquered the American Motorcycle Market

Previously, I talked a lot about adaptability and flexibility, and about being ready to adjust whenever possible. But blind adaptability is unlikely to help a company grow. Opportunities constantly arise in different areas, but should we take all of them?

I think the answer is obviously no. The next question, then, is how can we tell which opportunities to pursue and which to ignore? Should we be flexible in every dimension? Where should we be willing to change, and where should we stand firm?

This brings us to the vital importance of strategy. We cannot devote unlimited time, energy, and resources to evaluating every opportunity that comes our way. Nor can we expect every individual in a company to independently figure out where flexibility is appropriate and where it is not. These decisions cannot always be made in real time, simply by thinking on our feet.

As I mentioned earlier, strategy provides a solution. Put simply, it is a system of values and beliefs that guides a company's decisions. This system should be clear enough that everyone in the organization understands it and can use it to make decisions consistent with the company's direction.

Honda's experience in the American motorcycle market about 50 years ago provides a great example.

When Honda entered the American market, its larger motorcycles began experiencing mechanical problems because the team had not anticipated the differences in how American customers used them. Fortunately, some customers showed interest in Honda's smaller motorcycles.

Then Sears, the largest retailer in the United States at the time, offered to distribute Honda's motorcycles. Honda declined. This was a remarkable decision because the team was struggling to establish itself in a new market, and Sears seemed to offer an easy way to expand distribution.

Why would Honda turn down such an opportunity?

The team understood the importance of making its motorcycles reliable before expanding distribution. A major distribution deal could have created even greater problems if the motorcycles continued to break down. The team brought this lesson from its experience in Japan to the American market: the products had to work properly before distribution could expand.

Accepting a major distribution deal while the motorcycles were still experiencing problems might have seemed like an attractive shortcut, but it could have amplified the company's difficulties. Honda chose to address the underlying problem first rather than pursue immediate growth at the expense of product reliability. Reliability was something the company was not willing to compromise on simply to seize an opportunity.

Honda could also have used its low production costs to lower prices and position itself as a cheap motorcycle brand, much like Chevrolet in the American automobile market. But this would have conflicted with Honda's view of itself as a high-technology company. Rather than compete primarily on price, the team chose to focus on making its products reliable.

The team accepted the difficult situation, saved money during the winter, and sent the motorcycles back to Japan to address the problems. Instead of pursuing every available opportunity to gain a foothold in the American market, Honda concentrated on resolving the issues that threatened its long-term success.

These examples show that Honda was flexible in some areas but unwilling to compromise on others. The team understood that entering a new market required adjustments, but not every adjustment was desirable. Even under considerable pressure, it maintained certain priorities.

This raises an important question: which aspects of a company's strategy should managers be flexible about, and which should they be unwilling to change?

The answer relates to the company's activity system. The more central an activity is to a company's strategy, the more carefully managers should consider changing it. A company's activities do not operate in isolation. They support one another and collectively contribute to the company's strategic position. Changing one activity can therefore affect the effectiveness of others.

In Honda's case, product reliability was essential to building a sustainable position in the American market, while its technological identity influenced how it wanted to compete. Sacrificing these priorities simply to secure distribution or attract customers through lower prices could have undermined the direction the company was pursuing.

Of course, this does not mean that every aspect of a strategy must remain unchanged forever. Markets evolve, customer needs change, and strategies sometimes need to be reconsidered. The important distinction is between changing something because circumstances require it and changing it simply because an opportunity happens to be available.

Ultimately, adaptation is selective, not purely opportunistic. Managers should not change everything whenever a new opportunity appears. They need to understand which parts of their strategy can be adjusted and which are central to its success. A clear strategy provides the principles that help them make this distinction, even when the pressure to pursue an attractive opportunity is difficult to resist.
