*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

In January 2009, Tropicana, the market-leading orange juice brand in the US, redesigned its packaging almost completely. The orange with a straw became a plain glass of juice. The bold orange color became paler. The brand name turned vertical, the screw cap became a squeezable lid shaped like an orange, and the wording on the carton changed. The redesign and its campaign cost an estimated $30 to $50 million. The case gives you the list of changes and stops there, with no sales data.

I worked it cold.

## The central question

When the product inside doesn't change at all, how much of its value sits in the packaging customers recognize at a glance?

## Key ideas

My first reaction was about psychological attachment. People get used to the things they buy on autopilot, and a carton they've seen a hundred times works like a familiar face. I connected it to New Coke, where changing something people loved triggered a reaction far bigger than the change itself. That gave me the right family of risk before I looked at a single number.

I also raised the questions a good manager would ask before approving a redesign like this: had customers complained, was there evidence the old packaging hurt sales, and had the opportunity cost of $30 to $50 million been weighed. I treated those as open questions and judged the redesign on what the case actually showed.

The change I flagged as riskiest was the main image. The orange with a straw was distinctive and nobody else used it. A glass of juice is generic, since most competitors show one too. Tropicana swapped an asset only it owned for one the whole category shares. That mattered most in a category where people shop quickly and mostly by recognition.

The second concern was how many things changed at once. Image, color, layout, cap and wording all moved together. Loyal buyers need at least one or two familiar cues to hold on to. When every cue disappears, the carton can look like a different product, or a cheaper one, or one whose taste has changed.

I also called the size of the drop before seeing it. The real number was a sales decline of about 20% in the first two months, roughly $30 million in lost revenue in that window alone. That is close to the cost of the redesign campaign itself. PepsiCo brought the original packaging back quickly.

## My synthesis

I judged the decision reckless, not just unlucky. The risk was foreseeable without any formal research. Anyone who has watched a regular customer reach for a carton without reading it can see that changing everything at once puts that habit at risk. A bolder, more premium look was a reasonable goal. Keeping a few recognizable cues while updating the rest would have served it better.

## Connections

This connects to [Pepsi A.M.](/mba-lab/pepsi-am-and-the-room-that-already-knew). Both cases turn on a familiar habit: Pepsi underestimated the morning ritual around coffee, while Tropicana underestimated how much customers relied on familiar packaging to find their juice.

## Questions I still have

Is there a practical way to measure how much recognition a package carries before changing it, for example by testing how fast loyal buyers find it on a crowded shelf? And when a brand really does need to modernize, which elements should change first, and which should stay fixed until the new look has earned its own recognition?

## Final perspective

The redesign treated familiarity as clutter to be cleaned up, when for loyal buyers familiarity was the product's most useful feature. A modern, premium look is a fair goal. It just has to be reached without erasing the cues people use to find the brand.
