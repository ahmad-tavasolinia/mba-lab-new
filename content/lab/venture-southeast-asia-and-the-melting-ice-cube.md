*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

A private equity firm was weighing the acquisition of a small Thailand-based company with a niche rail-electrification technology. It operated across five countries and held 80% of a market growing at 10% a year. But that 80% share was shrinking by roughly ten points annually, as two competitors gained ground fast. The case gave me the full financials and asked for a valuation and a yes-or-no. No outcome was given, and no "right answer" was printed anywhere in the material.

My first instinct was caution. A business with an eroding position in a fast-moving technology sector reads as risky by default, before a single number is touched.

## Central question

When a calculation produces a clean, reassuring conclusion, is that a reason to trust it more, or to check it harder?

## Key ideas

Then I did the math, and I made an error. I applied the ten-point annual share decline as a relative drop: 80%, then 72%, then 64.8%. The correct version is a straight subtraction of ten points a year: 80%, then 70%, then 60%. The relative version, combined with a growing total market, produced a result that looked almost elegant. Profit stayed roughly flat while market share collapsed, because the market's growth offset the erosion. I built a real argument on that result before I caught the mistake and redid the five-year projection. The correct version showed profit falling by nearly half over five years.

The correction mattered less than what came after it. With the real numbers in front of me, the decision was no longer about the trend. It was about time value. A shrinking asset is not worth today's price. It is worth what its future cash flows are worth, given that money next year is worth less than money now, and even less for a business this volatile. I discounted the corrected five-year profit stream at a rate that reflected the real risk, much higher than a stable industry's rate. The present value came out well below what today's profit would suggest the business is worth. That gap is the whole case. It separates what a nervous buyer sees on the label from what the business is worth once its own trajectory is priced in honestly.

## My synthesis

My call was to buy, but only as a discount play, not a growth story. A shrinking company priced accordingly can still be a legitimate bet, the way a value investor buys something struggling because the price already reflects the struggle. That only works with a credible plan to stop the decline. A lower price does not turn yesterday's problems into tomorrow's bargain on its own.

The more important lesson sits underneath that call. The wrong answer and the right one differed by a single assumption about how a percentage decline compounds, and the wrong one was more satisfying to arrive at.

## Connections

This case sits with Pepsi A.M., Heinz, Bud Light, Real Burger World, and ESPN, but it moves the failure from persuasive reasoning to persuasive arithmetic. The pattern is the same: something felt right before it had been checked.

## Questions I still have

Is there a checking habit that would have caught a relative-versus-absolute error before it was built into a full argument, instead of after? And how much should the feel of a result, whether clean, reassuring, or elegant, count as a warning sign in itself, regardless of whether the math is later found to be wrong?

## Final perspective

The valuation method is fairly standard. What I want to keep is catching a real arithmetic error mid-argument, seeing it produce a reassuring conclusion, and discarding that conclusion once the correct numbers told a harder story. The elegant answer and the correct answer are not always the same, and the elegant one is easier to leave unchecked.
