*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

In the early 2000s, two friends quit their jobs, sold their apartments, and raised roughly £300,000 to build a "real" fast-food burger chain. The food was fresh, never frozen, with nothing pre-cooked, and it was positioned directly against McDonald's. They planned two flagship stores, then a franchise empire across the UK and Europe. The business closed within a few years.

The case describes the failure in detail: a lease signed too late, a manager with no fast-food experience, a menu designed by two people with no culinary background, and a second store opened before the first one turned a profit. I worked it cold, before reading the case's own diagnosis.

## Central question

When a company's own founding premise is what's breaking it operationally, why is the fix so rarely to change the premise?

## Key ideas

My first answer was "it wasn't well thought out." That is true, but too general to explain this specific failure. Pushing further, I landed on something sharper. The brand's own definition of "real" (nothing frozen, nothing pre-cooked, everything made fresh at order time) was in direct conflict with running a fast, profitable fast-food operation. A consultant had told them as much: precook or use frozen ingredients to fix the slow service. That advice would have solved the operational problem and broken the premise the company was named after.

The case then showed a second failure next to the first. The burgers, the core product, weren't good. The shakes and chips were, and some customers crossed over from a competitor across the street just to buy the drinks. My instinct was to pivot toward what was working, build a drinks brand, and drop what wasn't landing. Real businesses do this. Finding the unexpectedly successful piece of a struggling company and building outward from it is a legitimate strategic move.

The harder question was whether the founders could do that. Their identity, their funding pitch, and their company name were all built around "real burgers." Letting go of that to follow what was quietly succeeding takes discipline: treating commitment to the business as separate from attachment to the original idea. That is hard to do. Confusing the two is how a viable path gets ignored in favor of the one someone fell in love with first.

## My synthesis

The operational post-mortem is the less useful part of this case. Lease timing, hiring mistakes, and an under-tested menu appear in almost every startup failure. The more specific story is a founding premise that conflicted with the operational fix the business needed, next to a quietly viable alternative that the founders did not treat as their real company.

## Connections

This case sits with Pepsi A.M., Heinz, and Bud Light, each worked cold. In those cases, confidence, a dramatic number, or a correct-sounding overall verdict stood in for a checked argument. This one adds a founder-specific version: attachment to the founding idea standing in for a clear read of what the business had become.

## Questions I still have

How can I tell, from inside a company, whether I'm defending a sound premise with bad execution or a premise that is itself the operational problem? And is separating commitment to the business from attachment to its original idea a personal discipline, or does it take someone outside the founding team to force the split?

## Final perspective

The operational post-mortem, with its lease timing and hiring mistakes, is standard startup failure. What I want to keep is the reminder that founders sometimes sink a working business rather than admit the version they're attached to isn't the one that is succeeding.
