*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

A Korean company had just acquired a Boston biotech that held a chemical for speeding up fruit ripening, with the best results on apples: earlier harvest and better quality. The parent company wanted to know whether commercializing it made sense. The case gave no market data at the outset and no confirmation that the chemical had ever been tested outside a lab. It gave only the acquisition, already done, and a decision sitting on top of it.

## Central question

How do I tell an instinct that sounds true because it is comforting from one that actually holds up?

## Key ideas

The first friction was about scope, not strategy. My instinct was to question the acquisition itself: why buy a company you might not use? That is a fair question in the abstract and the wrong one here. The acquisition was already done. The decision in front of me was narrower: given that the company owns this chemical, commercialize or not. If I had confused the two, I would have spent the whole case arguing about a choice nobody was asking me to make.

With the scope fixed, I had very little data to lean on. A Korean parent company buying a Boston biotech working on a ripening chemical suggested that the real market was never Korea's own apple supply. It was the US market the biotech was already pursuing before the acquisition. That inference came from almost nothing, but this method allows it: when the facts are silent, reason to a plausible bridge.

The harder part came after I committed to "yes, commercialize." I was asked why now, and not after gathering more data. My first answer was a reflex: "sooner is better in food markets." That is true of nearly every business decision, so it is not really an answer. When I was pushed to name the actual mechanism, the real one surfaced. A harvest happens once a year. If you miss this season's window, there is no next-quarter retry, only a wait of a full year. That is a structural deadline built into the product category.

The numbers backed the argument once they arrived. A regional pilot in Maine apple orchards covered 8,000 acres. The chemical delivered over $600 in extra value per acre against a $50 cost to the farmer, mostly from an early-harvest price premium plus a modest yield increase. Even at a conservative 50% adoption rate in year one, the pilot alone generated real revenue.

## My synthesis

My call held: commercialize, on the condition that the seasonal argument is the real reason and not a slogan standing in for one. The ROI numbers were reassuring, but a pilot with good returns can seem to validate almost any decision after the fact. What made the reasoning sound was replacing "sooner is better" with the specific, checkable mechanism underneath it, before the numbers arrived.

## Connections

This case sits with Venture Southeast Asia, Real Burger World, Pepsi A.M., Old Spice, Heinz, ESPN, Burger King, and Bud Light. Each one showed a different way a plausible read can outrun what it proves. This one adds two: a true-sounding generality mistaken for a specific mechanism, and a scope error mistaken for a strategic insight.

## Questions I still have

Can I notice in the moment that a reflexive answer like "sooner is better" is a slogan and not a mechanism, or does that only show when someone pushes for a sharper reason? And how often does scope confusion, arguing against a decision already made instead of the narrower one being asked, derail an otherwise sound analysis?

## Final perspective

The ROI math was straightforward once the inputs were clean. What I want to keep is the difference between an instinct that sounds true and one that holds up. "Sooner is better" falls apart under scrutiny. "This market has a once-a-year window that doesn't wait for you" does not. Finding that difference usually took being pushed past my first, easier answer.
