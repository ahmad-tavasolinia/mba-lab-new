*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

In the late 1980s, Pepsi tested a product called Pepsi A.M.: regular Pepsi with 25% more caffeine, positioned as a coffee replacement for the morning. It passed regional testing and reached production and distribution readiness, then was killed before a national launch. The case puts you inside an internal Pepsi meeting from that time, mid-debate, with executives arguing both sides before the final call.

I worked it cold, before reading the transcript.

## Central question

How do I tell a confident argument from a correct one, especially when the confident voice has more authority in the room than I do?

## Key ideas

My instinct came fast: kill it. Coffee in the morning is a ritual, not a product choice, and rituals don't move on command just because a company wants them to. That was my whole call, made before I saw a single internal argument.

Then came the meeting transcript, with real people arguing the decision in real time. Susan pushed for the launch: "that's literally our job as marketers, to make it normal." She was confident and direct, but confidence is not an argument. She restated the goal as if restating it were a plan. Jessica made a sharper, narrower claim. Drinking Pepsi at breakfast might carry real social embarrassment, because coffee is a shared, visible morning ritual, and abandoning it in front of other people has a cost that a private taste preference doesn't.

Jessica and I had used the same word, ritual, from two directions, without borrowing it from each other. That suggested the word was doing real work.

Pepsi killed the product. A detail in a contemporary news report changed how I saw the case. Coca-Cola had the same insight, that people were drinking cola instead of coffee in the morning, and it solved it with almost nothing. It told people it was fine to drink a regular Coke in the morning. There was no new product, no added caffeine, and no new brand to build.

## My synthesis

My original call, that the ritual can't be broken, was right in direction but too absolute. The Coke comparison supports a narrower version: the ritual was not unbreakable. Pepsi broke it the hard way when an easy way was available. Being early and being right are different claims, and the Coke comparison is what sharpened mine.

## Questions I still have

Can I notice in real time that a confident restatement of the goal is being mistaken for an argument, or does that only show afterward, once an outside comparison exists? And when an insight can be acted on the hard way or the easy way, can I spot the easy version before committing to the hard one, instead of reconstructing it later from what a competitor did?

## Final perspective

Getting the verdict right early is not what made this exercise useful. What mattered was not letting confidence in the room stand in for a correct argument, including my own, which was right in direction but too absolute until the Coca-Cola comparison sharpened it.
