*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I’m practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

## The case

In April 2023, Bud Light ran a small paid partnership with a transgender influencer, tied to a personal milestone she was celebrating. She also received a custom can as a gift. It was not a national ad campaign, just a minor item in a much larger marketing calendar. The case describes this setup and stops there. It gives no sales figures and says nothing about how people reacted.

My job was to predict what happened next.

## The central question

> When a prediction turns out to be correct, how do I tell which parts of my reasoning the outcome confirmed, and which parts just happened to land nearby?

## My first read

A beer is a signal as well as a drink. Bud Light had spent decades building an identity around a masculine-coded social world: football, friend groups, a certain kind of humor. Most people never put this into words, but they know what a beer says about them when others see them drink it. That meaning doesn’t change because a marketing team decides it should. I called it a real mismatch. If you change what a brand means overnight, the people who hold that meaning feel something was taken from them.

Later I went further and called the campaign “fatal.” I predicted that transgender consumers wouldn’t buy the product either, reasoning that a group historically pushed out of traditionally masculine spaces would be put off by the product itself, not just the campaign. I had no evidence for this. It was a plausible mechanism, and the case gave no data on how that audience reacted.

## What happened

According to the case, sales fell 17% nationally within two weeks, and unit sales fell 21%. The backlash came from Bud Light’s existing customers, the traditional drinkers, who felt the brand had abandoned them. Comments from a marketing executive that came across as dismissive of those customers made it worse. The company then released a hastily made patriotic ad to win them back. Most people read it as insincere, and the damage grew.

## My synthesis

The half of my read about the old audience held up: they felt betrayed, and a brand’s meaning belongs partly to the people who hold it. The half about the new audience was never tested, because the data says nothing about how transgender consumers reacted. I treated the whole argument as confirmed when only part of it had been checked. Getting the direction of the backlash right does not validate every claim inside the same prediction.

## Connections

This case sits alongside two others in the series. In *Pepsi A.M.*, the lesson was that confidence in the room can’t stand in for a correct argument. In *Heinz*, it was that a dramatic but irrelevant number can’t stand in for evidence. Here, a correct overall verdict can hide a claim nobody checked.

## Questions I still have

What habit would have caught the unsupported claim before the data arrived, instead of afterward? And in real meetings, how often is a correct overall call treated as proof of every claim inside it, when nobody goes back to separate the parts?

## Final perspective

The marketing insight is the less useful part of this case. What I want to keep is the gap between predicting an outcome and knowing which part of my reasoning the outcome confirmed. In a real discussion, a classmate or professor would likely ask me to separate the two.

### Key ideas

- A beer works as a signal. Brands built around a specific social identity carry a meaning that doesn’t change just because a marketing team wants it to.
- A brand’s meaning belongs partly to the people who hold it. Changing it overnight can feel to them like something was taken away.
- A plausible mechanism is not evidence. A claim can sound reasonable and still be untested by the case in front of you.
- A correct overall prediction can hide an unsupported claim inside it, unless each part is checked separately.
- Claiming credit for a whole argument when only part of it was confirmed is an avoidable mistake.
