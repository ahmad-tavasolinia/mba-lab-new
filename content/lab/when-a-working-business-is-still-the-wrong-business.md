A conversation with Mohammad Hossein Naddaf, founder of Nimrou.

When its founders launched the platform, online food ordering was still relatively new. Birjand had little direct competition, and the team built the app and website themselves. On the first day, they received around twenty orders.

There was demand.

But years later, its co-founder reached a more interesting conclusion: a business can work and still be the wrong business to build.

## Demand is not enough

The first problem was the market itself. Birjand was a relatively small city, and the founders had assumed they could start locally and expand later. In practice, the environment limited access to customers, capital, talent and networks.

Then the competitive landscape changed.

Larger platforms entered with much greater financial resources. Food delivery also has a structural problem: customers are usually loyal to the restaurant, not the intermediary. If several apps offer the same food, customers can simply compare prices and discounts.

Nimrou could generate orders, but competing for those orders required spending more.

This exposed a distinction that is easy to miss when looking only at revenue:

**Demand is not enough. The economics of serving that demand matter.**

The founders kept the business running for years, including through COVID, but eventually the opportunity cost became difficult to ignore. The company could survive, yet the founders were putting years of work and capital into a relatively low-margin business.

## Choosing the right market and position

That changed how the co-founder thinks about entrepreneurship.

If he started again, he would spend much more time researching the environment before building the product. Where are the customers? Where is the capital? Where is the talent? What does competition look like? Is the market structurally attractive?

He might also choose a different position in the value chain. Instead of competing directly with large food-delivery platforms, he could build tools for restaurants themselves.

## Founder fit matters

There was another lesson that had little to do with technology: founder fit matters.

The co-founder is naturally technical. He enjoys solving technical and product problems but is less suited to finance, HR and organizational management. 

In retrospect, he would not necessarily choose himself as CEO.

That is an important realization. Starting a company does not mean every founder has to become a general manager.

## What AI changes—and what it doesn't

And AI may change this equation further.

He believes AI can make much smaller companies possible by allowing a few people to supervise systems that previously required much larger teams. For a technical founder, that is particularly attractive.

But AI does not solve the hardest strategic question.

It makes building easier.

It does not necessarily make choosing what to build easier.

## The question before building

That may be the most useful lesson from Nimrou.

The founders were capable of building the product. Customers wanted it. The company survived.

Yet the bigger question was whether the market, competitive structure, economics and founders were aligned well enough to make the business worth building.

That is a different question from product-market fit.

And perhaps it is the question worth asking before writing the first line of code.
