In 2010, Old Spice ran a Super Bowl ad that spoke almost entirely to women, "Hello, ladies", for a men's grooming brand trying to win over younger men who statistically have fewer girlfriends and wives doing their shopping for them than the older, already-loyal customers do. The case gave me the full setup and nothing about how it landed: no sales figures, no reception, just the ad's own internal logic sitting there, slightly contradictory on its face.

I worked it cold, and caught something real in the first pass. If the brand needs younger men and the ad's actual words are aimed at romantic partners, there's a mismatch baked into the strategy before a single viewer sees it, younger, less-settled men are less likely to have someone else doing their shopping.

## The central question

When a good explanation is already sitting on the table, why does it so often lose out to a more interesting-sounding invented one?

## Key ideas

The case offered one resolution I hadn't considered: mothers buy grooming products for teenage sons too, which widens the "buyer, not user" logic well past girlfriends and wives. I checked it against the ad itself and it didn't hold up, the actual line is "look at your man," unmistakably a partner talking to a partner, not a mother's frame at all. That correction back to the specifics of what was actually said, rather than what the strategy could theoretically justify, mattered more than accepting the tidy resolution would have.

Then the case offered a second, better explanation, not who the words are addressed to, but how far a genuinely funny, shareable Super Bowl ad travels regardless of who it's nominally speaking to. Sitting with that explanation for a moment would have resolved the whole tension cleanly. Instead I reached for something else: a theory that young men watching football would feel a kind of gendered anxiety from the ad and respond to that instead. It sounded clever in the moment. It wasn't supported by anything in the case, and there was already a better answer sitting one exchange earlier that I'd skipped past in favor of a more interesting-sounding one.

What actually happened: sales jumped 60% within three months, then 125% by six, and Old Spice became the top-selling men's body wash brand in the country. Notably, over half the online sharing of the ad came from women, which is the "buyer, not user" mechanism working exactly as designed, not the virality-to-men theory I'd reached for instead.

## My synthesis

Two different corrections happened in this case, and they're not the same kind. Catching the mother-buys-for-teenage-son explanation as unsupported by the ad's actual words was disciplined reading, going back to the source instead of accepting a plausible-sounding resolution. Reaching for the gendered-anxiety theory instead of sitting with the already-offered virality explanation was the opposite instinct, reaching past a correct, available answer because it felt less interesting to just agree with it.

## Questions I still have

Is there a way to notice, in the moment, that a better explanation was already offered and skipped past, or does that only become visible in hindsight, once the data confirms which explanation was actually right? And why does an invented, more interesting-sounding theory feel more satisfying to reach for than a simpler correct one that's already available?

## Final perspective

The real lesson in this one isn't about Old Spice. It's about the specific moment where a good, already-available explanation gets passed over for a more interesting-sounding invented one, under the pressure of needing to say something. Precision loses to cleverness more often than it should, and the fix isn't more creativity, it's engaging with the answer already on the table before reaching past it for a better story.
