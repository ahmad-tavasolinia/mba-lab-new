*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

In 2010, Old Spice ran a Super Bowl ad that spoke almost entirely to women: "Hello, ladies." It was for a men's grooming brand that wanted to win over younger men. Younger men statistically have fewer girlfriends and wives doing their shopping than the older, already-loyal customers do.

The case gave me the full setup and nothing about how the ad landed: no sales figures and no reception. It had only the ad's own logic, which looked slightly contradictory.

In the first pass I noticed a mismatch built into the strategy. If the brand needs younger men and the ad's words are aimed at romantic partners, the strategy has a problem before anyone watches. Younger, less settled men are less likely to have someone else doing their shopping.

## Central question

When a good explanation is already on the table, why does it so often lose out to a more interesting-sounding invented one?

## Key ideas

The case offered one resolution I hadn't considered: mothers buy grooming products for teenage sons too, which extends the "buyer, not user" logic beyond girlfriends and wives. I checked it against the ad itself and it didn't hold. The actual line is "look at your man," which is a partner talking to a partner, not a mother. Going back to what the ad actually says mattered more than accepting a tidy resolution.

Then the case offered a second explanation, and a better one. The point was not who the words were addressed to, but how far a funny, shareable Super Bowl ad travels regardless. That explanation resolved the whole tension. I didn't stay with it. Instead I proposed that young men watching football would feel a kind of gendered anxiety from the ad and respond to that. Nothing in the case supported this, and a better-supported explanation had been offered one exchange earlier.

According to the case, sales rose 60% within three months and 125% by six, and Old Spice became the top-selling men's body wash brand in the country. Over half the online sharing of the ad came from women. That fits the "buyer, not user" mechanism, and it doesn't fit my theory about young men.

## My synthesis

Two different corrections happened in this case. Rejecting the mother-buys-for-teenage-son explanation came from going back to the ad's actual words instead of accepting a plausible resolution. The second went the other way. I passed over a correct, available answer because agreeing with it felt less interesting than building my own.

## Connections

This case follows Pepsi A.M., Heinz, Bud Light, Real Burger World, ESPN, Venture Southeast Asia, and Burger King. Each one showed a different way a persuasive read can outrun what it proves. This one runs in the opposite direction from most of them: the problem here was too little confidence in a good idea that was already available, instead of too much confidence in a bad one.

## Questions I still have

Can I notice in the moment that a better explanation was already offered and skipped, or does that only show once the data confirms which one was right? And why does an invented, more interesting theory feel more satisfying to reach for than a simpler correct one that is already available?

## Final perspective

This case is less about Old Spice than about the moment a good, available explanation gets passed over for a more interesting one under pressure to say something. Precision loses to cleverness more often than it should. The fix is not more creativity. It is to engage with the answer already on the table before looking for a better story.
