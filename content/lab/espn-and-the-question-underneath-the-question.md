*How I work these cases: Before my MBA starts, I'm practicing the case method on my own. I give an AI a business case and ask it to release the details in stages, the way a class discussion unfolds. I give my read at each stage without looking anything up. Then the real outcome is revealed and I compare it with my reasoning. I call this working a case cold. These posts are my write-ups.*

By 2024, ESPN's math had stopped working the old way. Its cable subscriber base had once topped 100 million and had fallen to around 70 million, and it kept sliding. Live sports rights kept getting more expensive anyway.

Disney responded with three moves at once. It would keep earning from the cable business while it still worked. It would launch a direct-to-consumer streaming service in 2025. And, to the surprise of its own league partners, it would team up with two competitors, Fox and Warner Bros. Discovery, on a joint streaming bundle.

The case was written in mid-2024 and ends without saying which of these worked. I had to decide what ESPN should do.

## Central question

When a strategic choice is framed as a binary, protect the old business or accelerate into the new one, should I question the binary first?

## Key ideas

The framing I was given was binary: protect cable as long as possible, or accelerate into streaming. I chose streaming. My argument was habit formation. The longer people get used to watching sports on Amazon or Netflix, the harder it is to win them back. Waiting doesn't preserve options. It lets competitors finish converting the customers ESPN would later need to win back.

The pushback that mattered came from the numbers. ESPN's rights obligations were running above $10 billion a year. Cable, even while shrinking, was still its most profitable business by a wide margin. A streaming subscriber was worth a fraction of a cable household. "Accelerate" sounds clean in a sentence, but in the math it means paying for content twice: once for the audience you're trying to keep, and once for the audience you're trying to build, while the new revenue is still far too small to carry the cost.

I kept my position and made it more specific. I wasn't proposing to abandon cable. I was proposing to run both and push the streaming side hard instead of cautiously.

I also used an analogy, saying cable would die like the newspaper industry did: all at once, with no soft landing. When I was asked to defend it, I couldn't fully. Live sports has something newspapers didn't have, which is appointment viewing. People have a reason to watch in real time that a rerun or a text summary can't replace. I had used the analogy without checking it first.

The case doesn't cover what came next, so I looked it up afterward. The three-way joint venture collapsed in January 2025 under antitrust pressure, before it ever launched. With that backup gone, Disney had no soft option left. It pushed the standalone ESPN app forward and launched it in August 2025, timed to the start of NFL and college football season. That was the urgency I had argued for, but Disney got there because its backup plan collapsed, not because of a clean strategic choice.

## My synthesis

My final call held up reasonably well. The more useful lesson is one level below the verdict. A position is only worth holding under pressure if it gets sharper because of the pushback. Mine did: the numbers forced "accelerate" to mean something specific. The analogy did not get sharper. It broke, because it was never doing real analytical work. It stood in for an argument I hadn't finished building.

## Connections

This case belongs with Pepsi A.M., Heinz, Bud Light, and Real Burger World, each worked cold, each showing how confident-sounding reasoning can claim more than it proves. This one adds analogy to the list, next to misplaced confidence, dramatic but irrelevant evidence, and a correct verdict with unchecked claims inside it.

## Questions I still have

Can I test how much weight an analogy can carry before I use it, instead of finding its limits when someone pushes back? And when a real outcome matches my prediction, how do I tell whether my reasoning was right or whether outside events, like the joint venture collapsing, made the decision?

## Final perspective

The final call is not the part of this case I want to keep, even though it roughly matched what happened. What I want to keep is the reminder that an analogy can carry an argument further than the argument goes. Before I let a comparison do the persuading, I should check that it holds.
