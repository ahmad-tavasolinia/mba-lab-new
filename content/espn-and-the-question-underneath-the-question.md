By 2024, ESPN's math had stopped working the old way. A cable subscriber base that once topped 100 million had fallen to around 70 million and kept sliding, while live sports rights kept getting more expensive regardless. Disney's answer was three moves at once: keep milking the cable business while it still worked, push a direct-to-consumer streaming launch for 2025, and, surprising even their own league partners, team up with two competitors, Fox and Warner Bros. Discovery, on a joint streaming bundle. The case, written mid-2024, ends without telling you which of these actually worked.

I worked it cold.

## The central question

When a strategic choice is framed as a binary, protect the old business or accelerate into the new one, is the binary itself the thing worth questioning first?

## Key ideas

The framing I was given was binary: protect cable as long as possible, or accelerate into streaming. I picked streaming, and defended it on a mechanism I hadn't seen named directly in the case, habit formation. The longer someone gets used to watching sports on Amazon or Netflix, the harder they are to pull back later. Waiting doesn't preserve optionality. It just lets competitors finish converting the customers ESPN would need to win back.

The pushback that actually mattered came from the numbers, not the concept. ESPN's rights obligations were running past $10 billion a year. Cable, even shrinking, was still their most profitable business by a wide margin. Streaming subscribers were worth a fraction of what a cable household was worth. "Accelerate" sounds clean in a sentence. In the actual math, it means paying for content twice over, once for the audience you're trying to keep, once for the audience you're trying to build, while the new revenue stream isn't remotely large enough yet to carry the weight.

I held the position, but sharpened what it actually meant: not abandon cable, run both, push the streaming side hard rather than cautiously. Then I reached for an analogy, cable dying like the newspaper industry did, all at once, no soft landing, and got asked to defend it. I couldn't, not fully. Live sports has something newspapers didn't: appointment viewing, a reason to watch in real time that a rerun or a text summary can't replace. The analogy was doing more rhetorical work than analytical work, and I hadn't checked it before reaching for it.

What happened next wasn't in the case, so I went looking afterward. The three-way streaming joint venture collapsed in January 2025 under antitrust pressure before it ever launched. With that hedge gone, Disney had no soft option left, they pushed the standalone ESPN app forward and launched it that August, timed deliberately to the start of NFL and college football season, leaning hard into exactly the urgency I'd argued for. Forced there, in the end, by the collapse of their own backup plan rather than a clean strategic choice.

## My synthesis

The final call held up reasonably well, but the more useful lesson sits one level below the verdict. Holding a position under pressure only counts for something if the position gets sharper because of the pushback, mine did, once the numbers forced "accelerate" to mean something more specific than the word implied on its own. The analogy, by contrast, didn't get sharper under pressure. It broke, because it was never actually load-bearing, it was borrowed drama standing in for an argument I hadn't fully built yet.

## Questions I still have

Is there a way to test an analogy's load-bearing weight before using it, rather than only discovering its limits when someone else pushes back on it? And when a real-world outcome ends up matching a prediction, how do you tell whether the reasoning was actually right, or whether external events, like a joint venture collapsing, did the deciding instead?

## Final perspective

The part of this case worth keeping isn't the final call, which turned out to roughly match what happened. It's the reminder that a good analogy can carry an argument further than the argument actually goes, and it's worth stopping to check whether the comparison holds before letting it do the persuading for you.
